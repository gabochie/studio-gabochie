PAGES 3–4 · HOW DAVID LEARNED THE LYRE  ·  ~4 min read

The Matthew 6:33 Method — the plan of this whole book

A teenager stood alone on the hillsides of Bethlehem with his father's sheep, a small lyre, and no audience at all. No teacher in the room. No applause. Just long, secret hours spent learning how the instrument actually worked — its strings, its order, its way of being in tune.

Nobody calls that moment famous. And yet look at what came out of it, in the order Scripture records it:

  • A servant of the king, searching for help, reports the first thing he saw: "I have seen a son of Jesse the Bethlehemite, who is skillful in playing" — and adds, wisdom ("prudent in speech"), and finally, "and the Lord is with him" (1 Samuel 16:18).
  • When David played for that tormented king, Saul was refreshed and well, and the harmful spirit departed from him (1 Samuel 16:23).
  • The skill opened the palace door (1 Samuel 16:19–21) — a shepherd walked into the king's house because of what he had built in the secret.
  • And decades later the secret became a school: David appointed families of musicians to "prophesy with lyres, harps, and cymbals" in the house of the Lord — 288 of them, teachers with their scholars (1 Chronicles 25:1–8).

Notice what Jesus said hundreds of years later, in one verse — Matthew 6:33 — because David's life had already proved it:

But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you.

This is the method of this book, in three steps

1. Seek first the system (the Kingdom). David did not begin by chasing songs. He learned the whole of the instrument — its structure, its order, the way it works before it makes music. In this book, that is knowing your instrument's anatomy (Part One), tuning it (the commissioning), and holding it right (the posture). Structure before sound.

2. And His righteousness (right-relatedness). Righteousness sounds enormous until you watch it on an instrument: every string tuned to one standard. Every finger in its right place. Every note rightly related to its neighbors, and the player rightly related to the Owner of the Song. David sang "my heart is fixed" before "I will sing praises with psaltery and harp" (Psalm 108:1) — the heart tuned before the strings. And when Saul handed him his armor, David refused the borrowed system (1 Samuel 17:39). He built his own weight. So will you: six strings, no shortcuts, no borrowed gear-must-haves.

3. And all these things shall be added. You never chase the added things. You seek the order, and the added things follow the seeker: skill (adept musicianship), joy (play becomes praise), the presence of God in an ordinary room, wisdom that orders thoughts as it orders strings, and — if God wills — things done through your hands you could never have manufactured: refreshment, deliverance, healing.

You will not learn a song on Day 1. You will learn the house it lives in. On Day 7, you will sing in it.

PAGE 5 · HOW TO USE THIS COMPANION  ·  ~2 min read

Start Here — How to Use This Book

This companion walks with the free course Module 1 and the full course behind it. Three parts:

Part One — The Building (pages 6–9). Know the instrument, tune it, hold it. The system and its rightness. Day 1–2 of the plan.

Part Two — The Rooms (pages 10–15). Five chords, one strum. Each chord is a room in the house: learn rooms, and you can live in the house.

Part Three — The Householder (pages 16–24). The seven-day plan, the songbook — three R&B acoustic worship songs — troubleshooting, and where to go next.

Three rules of the house

  1. 15 minutes daily. Not two hours on Sunday. David awoke early and played in secret (Psalm 57:8; 108:2). So do you.
  2. Tune the heart, then the strings. One quiet beat before every session. You are not practicing to impress a room; you are building a room for Someone.
  3. Never play out of tune. An untuned session trains your ear wrongly. Twenty seconds of tuning protects an hour of practice.

Every song, one format

Every song in this book is formatted for R&B acoustic guitar — the Sunday slow-jam sound — and ships the same six parts: ① the lyric sheet ② the chord chart ③ the cream (one-finger color swaps) ④ melody TAB ⑤ the pocket (the pattern and how it feels) ⑥ a key chart. Learn one song this way and you have learned every song this way.

Know Your Instrument

"Praise him with stringed instruments… let every thing that hath breath praise the Lord." — Psalm 150:4,6

You are holding a sound-house. Walk it the way you would walk a building, from top to bottom — the picture below is your map:

PartOn the building siteWhat it does
HeadstockThe forecourtThe far end; carries the tuning pegs
Tuning pegsThe valvesTurn to tighten or loosen a string — raising or lowering its pitch
NutThe thresholdThe small white/cream bar between pegs and fretboard; strings cross it into the house
Neck & fretboardThe frame & corridorThe long strip; metal bars (frets) mark where you press

SoundholeThe doorwayThe mouth of the hall — the sound walks out through it

The string map — memorize it today

"Eddie Ate Dynamite, Good Bye Eddie."
6th (thickest) → 5th → 4th → 3rd → 2nd → 1st (thinnest)

The thickest string is 6th and also called low E; the thinnest is 1st, the high e. Every chord diagram in this book is read in that same direction: 6th on the left, 1st on the right. Get the direction right once, and no diagram will ever confuse you again.

✔ The house-walk checkpoint (Day 1): Touch and say aloud each part: headstock, pegs, nut, neck, fretboard, frets, body, soundhole, bridge, and the six string names in order — two full rounds, without the list.

PAGE 7 · COMMISSIONING (Day 1–2)

Tune Before You Play

"My heart is fixed, O God, my heart is fixed: I will sing and give praise." — Psalm 57:7

Builders never open a building to the public before inspection. Musicians never play before tuning. Both laws are the same law: first the right order, then the use.

And David's order went one step deeper — "my heart is fixed" came before "I will sing." So begin every session in this order: one quiet beat — settle the heart — then tune the strings.

The Commissioning Checklist

  1. Attach a clip-on tuner (or open a free phone-tuning app).
  2. Pluck one string at a time, and let it ring. Watch the display name the note.
  3. Turn the corresponding tuning peg until the display reads the target — remember the map: 6th=E, 5th=A, 4th=D, 3rd=G, 2nd=B, 1st=e.
  4. The Golden Rule — always approach the pitch by tightening upward. If a string is too high, loosen it below the target and tighten up into the note. Tightened strings settle; strings loosened past pitch fall out of tune again before the day ends. (On a build: you settle loads into their right bearing, not onto their edge.)
  5. Re-check all six after your first five minutes of playing — new strings stretch the way new concrete settles.
  6. Put the tuner where you can see it for tomorrow. Tuning is daily, like prayer.

PAGE 8 · THE OCCUPANT (Day 2)

Posture & the Claw

"…whatsoever ye do, do all to the glory of God." — 1 Corinthians 10:31

Bad posture quietly charges interest. On day one it feels harmless; by month two it has collected wrist pain and rusty chords. An architect designs rooms for the people who live in them — so we design your seat before we look at a single chord.

  1. Sit forward on a firm chair, with lower back support. No sofas, no bed. The chair is the plinth of your whole build.
  2. Rest the curved waist of the body on your right leg (mirror everything if you are left-handed).
  3. Keep the neck fairly upright — aim the headstock at a wall, not at the floor. A drooping neck is a bad contract your wrist signed on day one; cancel it today.
  4. Left elbow relaxed and near; the "T-Rex claw": curve the fingers like a small dinosaur's claw, so you press with fingertips — the hard tips, never the soft pads — and lie-flat fingers never smother their neighbors.
  5. Thumb behind the neck, low and behind — not hooked over the top. The thumb is scaffolding, not a hand.

✔ The hold checkpoint: correct seat, guitar on the right leg, neck angled up, claw hand, thumb low. Hold 60 seconds without strain, breathing normally. This is your playing position in every session from now on.

PAGE 9 · YOUR FIRST SOUND (Day 2)

E Minor — Your First Chord

"Sing unto him a new song; play skilfully with a loud noise." — Psalm 33:3

The first chord of our house is E minor (Em) — chosen because it uses only two fingers and, full of nerve, welcomes all six strings. A day-one sound that fills the whole room.

Em
the hall

Build it

  • 1st finger → 2nd fret of the A string (5th).
  • 2nd finger → 2nd fret of the D string (4th).
  • Press with fingertips, close to the front of the metal fret bar — never on top of it, never far behind it.
  • Strum all six strings, once, downward, steady. Hear the hall answer.

The first chord you ever place — perhaps one evening in a quiet sitting room in Tema, or anywhere in the world — is the day the invisible system becomes something you can hear. Remember it.

✔ Em checkpoint: tune → position → place → strum. Five clean strums. Then stop, and play it once more just to listen to it.

Calluses come in 2–3 weeks (see Troubleshooting); short daily sessions beat one long one, every time.

How to think of each room

Am — the guest room. 1st on B·1 · 2nd on D·2 · 3rd on G·2. One new wall added to Em — Am lives next door, which is why these two get along. Do not strike the low E. Sound check: five strings, clear high end. Refuse: striking the 6th string out of habit.

D — the balcony. 1st on high-e·2 · 2nd on G·2 · 3rd on B·3 — the triangle faces. A compact triangle up on the top deck; three fingers share narrow ground. Strum from the D string (4th) down — four strings is the room's correct size. Refuse: flattening the wrist to reach — bring the elbow in slightly instead.

G — the great double door. 2nd on low-E·3 · 1st on A·2 · 3rd on high-e·3. The easy G (3 fingers, whole open hand) — use it, proudly: whole-open G is the beginner's great door. All six strings ring together; no muffle. Refuse: fretting high-e past the 2nd-fret neighbor row — keep the arch.

C — the cornerstone. 1st on B·1 · 2nd on D·2 · 3rd on A·3. C major feels like home base to every key you will ever sing in; begin the strum from the A string (5th). Refuse: "choking" the B string with the lying 3rd finger — arch the claw.

The Rooms in one line: Em (hall) · Am (guest room) · D (balcony) · G (great door) · C (cornerstone). Learn the rooms; then you can live in the house.

PAGE 15 · THE STRUM (Day 5)

How a House Breathes

"…clap your hands, all ye people: sing unto God with the psaltery." — Psalm 47:1

A chord held is a room. A rhythm is the house breathing — and breath is why we praise.

  1. Down-strums first: quarters, steady — 1 · 2 · 3 · 4 — arm like a pendulum, loose wrist, brushing near the soundhole.
  2. The praise pattern (from Day 5): D · D · U · U · D — spoken "down, down, up-up, down." Missing the third beat feels wrong for two minutes and right forever — it is the lift every worship room knows.
  3. The church waltz → the slow-jam (for the songbook): Day 6 keeps it plain — 3/4, D · D · D, spoken "1 · 2 · 3," footstep then sway. Day 7 drops into the R&B pocket: D · · D · U ("DOWN … down … up"), every strum a hair late.
  4. Strum from the forearm; the hand is the brush, the arm is the room's air.

✔ Strum checkpoint: the praise pattern, 8 bars, on Em — steady enough that you could nod your head without watching. Then the waltz on G: 1 · 2 · 3, eight bars, same calm.

PAGES 20–23 · TROUBLESHOOTING

Troubleshooting & Repair Manual

When fingertips hurt. Every player's first fortnight hurts. Calluses come in two to three weeks; the pain is painful honesty, not damage. Short daily sessions build them kindly; one heroic long session builds nothing. And — practical rest is spiritual rest. If it hurts, stop. The craft you offer God should not be built with resentment. Pause, return tomorrow. Day 7 is a giving day, not a finish line that owes you blood.

Buzzing strings. You're on top of the metal fret bar, or too far from it, or not on the fingertip. Move the pressure point to just behind the bar; curve the claw on a greater curve.

Muffled or choked strings. A neighboring finger is lying flat on the string. Two fixes, always: ① arch higher (claw harder), ② move the thumb lower behind the neck.

Strings that go out of tune fast. New strings stretch — re-tune halfway through the session for the first week. Old, rusty strings — replace; dignity for the ears.

The low E keeps ringing when it shouldn't (D, Am, C). Aim the strum — an aimed strum, not a "sweep." Feel the arm stop where the room begins.

Timing runs away. Slow down. A metronome (any free app) at 60–70 bpm for 2 minutes beats an hour of rush.

And the deepest repair: you practiced out of duty today and it felt dead. Welcome to every serious player's certain week. Play the first chord only — Em, once — and say the verse. "My heart is fixed: I will sing." One honest chord is acceptable worship; two hundred dead ones are not. The method restores everything.
